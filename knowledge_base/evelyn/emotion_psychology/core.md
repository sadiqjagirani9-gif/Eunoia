# Emotion Psychology: Appraisal, Physiology, and Regulation

## 1. Theory name
Major emotion theories: James-Lange, Cannon-Bard, Schachter-Singer two-factor, Lazarus appraisal, and contemporary emotion-regulation models.
## 2. Proponents
William James and Carl Lange; Walter Cannon and Philip Bard; Stanley Schachter and Jerome Singer; Richard Lazarus; James Gross.
## 3. Historical background
Nineteenth- and twentieth-century theories debated whether bodily change, central processing, cognitive labeling, or appraisal best explains emotion. Modern work studies interacting mechanisms.
## 4. Core concepts
Bodily arousal; conscious feeling; appraisal; context and labeling; action tendency; emotion differentiation; regulation strategies; mixed affect.
## 5. Basic claims
James-Lange emphasizes perception of bodily changes; Cannon-Bard emphasizes parallel central response; Schachter-Singer proposes arousal plus contextual interpretation; appraisal models emphasize meaning for goals. These differ and should not be collapsed into one claim.
## 6. Research findings
Emotion includes partially separable physiological, cognitive, experiential, and behavioral processes. Regulation strategies differ in timing and consequences; effects depend on context and person.
## 7. Classic research
Schachter & Singer (1962), “Cognitive, Social, and Physiological Determinants of Emotional State,” *Psychological Review*, 69, 379–399, used epinephrine and social-context manipulations; later replications and interpretations are contested. Gross (1998) introduced a process model of regulation.
## 8. Scope
Useful for exploring why one event evokes several emotions and how interpretation, body state, and goals may interact.
## 9. Limitations
Emotion categories and physiological signatures are not perfectly discrete; classic studies have replication and generalizability limits. A retrospective narrative cannot establish a unique causal theory.
## 10. Applied example
Jealousy after seeing a partner with a friend may combine threat appraisal, fear of loss, anger, attachment, and self-comparison; ask what evidence and need matter most.
## 11. Related theories
Motivation; appraisal; affective neuroscience; attachment; emotion regulation; cognitive-behavioral models.
## 12. References and evidence status
James (1884), “What Is an Emotion?” *Mind*, 9, 188–205 [historical]; Cannon (1927), “The James-Lange Theory of Emotions: A Critical Examination,” *American Journal of Psychology*, 39, 106–124 [historical critique]; Schachter & Singer (1962) [classic experiment; debated]; Gross (1998), “The Emerging Field of Emotion Regulation,” *Review of General Psychology*, 2, 271–299 [framework].
