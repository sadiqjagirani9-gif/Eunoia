# Social Psychology: Attribution, Comparison, and Influence

## 1. Theory name
Attribution theory, social comparison, social influence, and interpersonal perception.
## 2. Proponents
Fritz Heider; Leon Festinger; Harold Kelley; Solomon Asch; contemporary social psychologists.
## 3. Historical background
Social psychology developed as an experimental study of how judgments and behavior change in social contexts, especially during the twentieth century.
## 4. Core concepts
Person and situation attribution; covariation; social comparison; conformity; impression formation; norms; trust; group identity.
## 5. Basic claims
People infer causes from incomplete evidence and compare themselves with others. Situational constraints can be underweighted when explaining behavior; norms and group context shape conduct without determining it.
## 6. Research findings
Classic effects show that social context can influence judgments and behavior, but effect size, replication, culture, and task design matter. The fundamental attribution error is a tendency, not a universal rule.
## 7. Classic research
Asch (1951), “Effects of Group Pressure upon the Modification and Distortion of Judgments,” studied line judgments under group pressure. Festinger (1954), “A Theory of Social Comparison Processes,” *Human Relations*, 7, 117–140, proposed comparison processes.
## 8. Scope
Useful for interpreting ambiguous behavior, social comparison, perceived rejection, group belonging, and interpersonal expectations.
## 9. Limitations
Laboratory conformity tasks do not represent all real-world settings; cultural norms differ. Attribution labels do not prove motive or excuse harmful behavior.
## 10. Applied example
If a colleague is quiet, compare situational explanations (workload, uncertainty, norms) with personal explanations before concluding hostility; seek observable evidence.
## 11. Related theories
Social identity; impression formation; self-presentation; relationship science; cognitive bias research.
## 12. References and evidence status
Heider (1958), *The Psychology of Interpersonal Relations* [foundational theory]; Festinger (1954) [theoretical paper]; Asch (1951) [classic experiment]; Gilbert & Malone (1995), “The Correspondence Bias,” *Psychological Bulletin*, 117, 21–38 [review].
