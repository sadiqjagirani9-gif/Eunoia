# Psychoanalysis and Psychodynamic Approaches

## 1. Theory name
Classical psychoanalysis, ego psychology, object relations, and contemporary psychodynamic approaches.
## 2. Proponents
Sigmund Freud; Anna Freud; Melanie Klein; Donald Winnicott; later theorists include Nancy McWilliams and Peter Fonagy.
## 3. Historical background
Freud developed psychoanalysis in late nineteenth-century Vienna. Later schools revised its ideas about development, defense, relationships, and the self; they are not one uniform theory.
## 4. Core concepts
Unconscious processes; conflict; defense; transference; internalized relationships; identity; mentalization. Anna Freud systematized defenses; Klein and Winnicott developed object-relational accounts.
## 5. Basic claims
Some motives and conflicts may be outside awareness; repeated relational expectations can shape present experience. These are interpretive hypotheses, not direct observations of a hidden mechanism.
## 6. Research findings
Some psychodynamic therapies show benefit for certain clinical problems in outcome research, while effect sizes, populations, and mechanisms vary. This does not validate every classical claim or justify amateur diagnosis.
## 7. Classic research
Freud's clinical case histories are historically influential but are not controlled experiments. Shedler (2010) reviewed evidence on psychodynamic therapy; later meta-analyses assess outcomes with methodological qualifications.
## 8. Scope
Can offer questions about conflict, defenses, repeated relational expectations, and meanings when the user finds this lens useful.
## 9. Limitations
Many classical constructs are difficult to operationalize or falsify; case material is vulnerable to selection and interpretation bias. Avoid asserting repression, projection, trauma, or childhood causation without evidence.
## 10. Applied example
If someone jokes whenever they feel vulnerable, consider humor as one possible way to regulate exposure, but also consider habit, culture, or simply playfulness; ask which account fits.
## 11. Related theories
Attachment; mentalization; interpersonal theory; cognitive-behavioral accounts; narrative identity.
## 12. References and evidence status
Freud (1915), “The Unconscious” [historical theory]; A. Freud (1936), *The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence* [historical theory]; Winnicott (1965), *The Maturational Processes and the Facilitating Environment* [clinical theory]; Shedler (2010), “The Efficacy of Psychodynamic Psychotherapy,” *American Psychologist*, 65(2), 98–109 [review; scope debated]. Classical mechanisms are historically important and variably supported.
