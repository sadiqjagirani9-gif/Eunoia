# Political and Social Philosophy: Freedom, Equality, and Power

## 1. Theory name
Social contract, liberalism, communitarianism, conservatism, justice, legitimacy, and power.
## 2. Proponents
Thomas Hobbes; John Locke; Jean-Jacques Rousseau; John Rawls; Robert Nozick; Michael Sandel; Hannah Arendt.
## 3. Historical background
Modern political philosophy developed around authority, rights, sovereignty, and social contract; contemporary work debates justice, equality, liberty, and community.
## 4. Core concepts
Legitimacy; consent; liberty; equality; rights; public goods; institutions; social structure; power; distributive and procedural justice.
## 5. Basic claims
Political theories propose different principles for legitimate institutions. Normative judgments (what should be) must be separated from empirical claims (what is and what causes outcomes).
## 6. Research findings
Empirical political science and sociology can estimate institutional effects, but normative conclusions require explicit values. Correlation across societies does not identify a policy's causal effect without design and assumptions.
## 7. Classic research
Hobbes (1651), *Leviathan*; Rousseau (1762), *The Social Contract*; Rawls (1971), *A Theory of Justice*. These are primary philosophical texts.
## 8. Scope
Useful for discussing personal choices shaped by institutions, rights, collective goods, social norms, and unequal resources.
## 9. Limitations
Political traditions are internally diverse and context-dependent. Avoid reducing complex ideologies to caricatures or presenting contested values as consensus.
## 10. Applied example
For workplace fairness, distinguish a factual question about pay distribution from normative questions about equal treatment, need, merit, and bargaining power.
## 11. Related theories
Ethics; sociology; economics; legal philosophy; democratic theory.
## 12. References and evidence status
Hobbes (1651), *Leviathan* [historical political theory]; Rousseau (1762), *The Social Contract* [normative theory]; Rawls (1971), *A Theory of Justice* [normative framework]; Nozick (1974), *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* [libertarian theory]. Competing positions remain live debates.
