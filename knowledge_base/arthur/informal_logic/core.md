# Informal Logic and Critical Thinking

## 1. Theory name
Informal logic, argumentation theory, and fallacy analysis.
## 2. Proponents
Stephen Toulmin; Chaïm Perelman; Douglas Walton; Ralph Johnson and J. Anthony Blair.
## 3. Historical background
Twentieth-century argumentation scholars renewed attention to ordinary-language reasoning, context, dialogue, and practical argument assessment.
## 4. Core concepts
Claim, grounds, warrant, backing, qualifier, rebuttal; relevance; acceptability; sufficiency; burden of proof; ad hominem, straw man, false dilemma, slippery slope, circularity.
## 5. Basic claims
Arguments should be assessed by their premises, inferential link, relevance, and context. A fallacy label is useful only when the defective step is explained.
## 6. Research findings
Argument evaluation depends on domain knowledge, framing, and cognitive load. Fallacy taxonomies are teaching aids; ordinary arguments may be ambiguous or mixed.
## 7. Classic research
Toulmin (1958), *The Uses of Argument*, models practical warrants and rebuttals. Walton (1996), *Arguments from Ignorance*, analyzes a commonly misused inference.
## 8. Scope
Useful for examining online claims, personal reasoning, public debate, and competing explanations.
## 9. Limitations
Not every weak argument fits a named fallacy. A flawed argument does not prove its conclusion false; an argument from authority can be reasonable when expertise is relevant and reliable.
## 10. Applied example
“My friend succeeded after quitting, so quitting always works” overgeneralizes from one case; identify sampling limits and what broader evidence is needed.
## 11. Related theories
Formal logic; rhetoric; Bayesian reasoning; scientific reasoning; argumentation schemes.
## 12. References and evidence status
Toulmin (1958), *The Uses of Argument* [framework]; Johnson & Blair (2006), *Logical Self-Defense* [textbook]; Walton (1996), *Arguments from Ignorance* [analysis]. Fallacy names classify patterns, not settled verdicts without context.
