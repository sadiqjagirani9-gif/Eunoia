# Formal Logic: Validity, Soundness, and Argument Forms

## 1. Theory name
Classical propositional and first-order predicate logic.
## 2. Proponents
Aristotle; George Boole; Gottlob Frege; Alfred Tarski; contemporary logicians.
## 3. Historical background
Aristotle's syllogistic was systematized in antiquity; mathematical logic expanded in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, especially through Boole and Frege.
## 4. Core concepts
Propositions, connectives, quantifiers, entailment, equivalence, validity, satisfiability, soundness, necessary and sufficient conditions.
## 5. Basic claims
Validity concerns whether premises entail a conclusion; soundness additionally requires true premises. A valid form can have false premises and a false conclusion.
## 6. Research findings
Formal systems precisely model selected inferential structures. They do not by themselves establish empirical premises or determine values and relevance.
## 7. Classic research
Frege's *Begriffsschrift* (1879) introduced a powerful formal notation; Tarski (1936) developed a semantic account of logical consequence. These are foundational works, not experiments.
## 8. Scope
Useful for clarifying conditional claims, contradiction, quantifier scope, and whether a conclusion follows from stated premises.
## 9. Limitations
Natural-language arguments are often enthymematic and ambiguous. Formal validity does not guarantee plausible assumptions or practical wisdom.
## 10. Applied example
“If A then B; A; therefore B” is valid modus ponens. “If A then B; B; therefore A” affirms the consequent and is invalid.
## 11. Related theories
Informal logic; proof theory; model theory; probability; decision theory.
## 12. References and evidence status
Aristotle, *Prior Analytics* [historical]; Frege (1879), *Begriffsschrift* [foundational formal logic]; Tarski (1936), “On the Concept of Logical Consequence” [semantic theory]. Formal results are exact relative to a specified system and premises.
