# Developmental Psychology: Change Across the Lifespan

## 1. Theory name
Lifespan development, psychosocial development, cognitive development, and identity formation.
## 2. Proponents
Jean Piaget; Erik Erikson; Lev Vygotsky; Urie Bronfenbrenner; contemporary lifespan researchers.
## 3. Historical background
Developmental psychology expanded from child study to lifespan, sociocultural, ecological, and longitudinal approaches.
## 4. Core concepts
Continuity and change; maturation; learning; identity; transitions; context; reciprocal person-environment influence; sensitive periods.
## 5. Basic claims
Development is shaped by biological, relational, cultural, and historical conditions. Stage theories are useful organizing frameworks but do not describe every individual uniformly.
## 6. Research findings
Longitudinal studies document both continuity and change. Timing, cohort, culture, and life events influence trajectories; retrospective accounts are incomplete.
## 7. Classic research
Bronfenbrenner (1979), *The Ecology of Human Development*; Erikson (1950), *Childhood and Society*; Vygotsky (1978), *Mind in Society*.
## 8. Scope
Useful for considering how values, identity, and coping can change across transitions without calling change inconsistency.
## 9. Limitations
Stage models can be culturally narrow or overgeneralized; age alone does not establish developmental task or maturity.
## 10. Applied example
For a shift toward wanting stability, consider new responsibilities, relationships, resources, and changing priorities rather than treating it as regression or contradiction.
## 11. Related theories
Personality development; attachment; identity; ecological systems; education.
## 12. References and evidence status
Erikson (1950) [historical psychosocial theory]; Bronfenbrenner (1979) [ecological framework]; Vygotsky (1978) [sociocultural theory]. Interpretive frameworks with varying empirical operationalization.
