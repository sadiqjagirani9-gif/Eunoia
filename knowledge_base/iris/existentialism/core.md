# Existentialism: Freedom, Choice, Anxiety, and Meaning

## 1. Theory name
Existential philosophy and meaning-centered approaches.
## 2. Proponents
Søren Kierkegaard; Friedrich Nietzsche; Martin Heidegger; Jean-Paul Sartre; Albert Camus; Viktor Frankl (related meaning-centered clinical thought).
## 3. Historical background
Nineteenth- and twentieth-century thinkers addressed individual existence, freedom, responsibility, finitude, alienation, and meaning amid social and intellectual change.
## 4. Core concepts
Choice; responsibility; anxiety; authenticity; finitude; absurdity; value creation; being-in-the-world; meaning.
## 5. Basic claims
Existential traditions differ. They often treat human life as involving situated choice and responsibility, while acknowledging limits, ambiguity, and the absence of guaranteed meaning.
## 6. Research findings
Existentialism is philosophical, not a unified empirical psychology. Meaning-centered interventions have separate clinical research and must not be conflated with every existential thesis.
## 7. Classic research
Sartre (1946), “Existentialism Is a Humanism”; Camus (1942), *The Myth of Sisyphus*; Frankl (1946), *Man's Search for Meaning*. These are philosophical or autobiographical works, not controlled experiments.
## 8. Scope
Useful for reflecting on values, responsibility, freedom under constraint, loneliness, and chosen meaning.
## 9. Limitations
Can romanticize suffering or overstate individual freedom; structural constraints and care obligations matter. Do not turn philosophy into prescriptive certainty.
## 10. Applied example
When a person asks what career is meaningful, explore what they value and choose while acknowledging income, health, caregiving, and opportunity limits.
## 11. Related theories
Phenomenology; ethics; identity; self-determination; meaning in life research.
## 12. References and evidence status
Kierkegaard (1843), *Either/Or*; Nietzsche (1887), *On the Genealogy of Morality*; Heidegger (1927), *Being and Time*; Sartre (1943), *Being and Nothingness*; Camus (1942), *The Myth of Sisyphus*; Frankl (1946) [philosophical/historical texts; not one empirical theory].
