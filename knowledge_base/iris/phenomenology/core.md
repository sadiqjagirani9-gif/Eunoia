# Phenomenology: Lived Experience and Meaning

## 1. Theory name
Phenomenology as philosophical method and qualitative research tradition.
## 2. Proponents
Edmund Husserl; Martin Heidegger; Maurice Merleau-Ponty; Alfred Schutz.
## 3. Historical background
Husserl developed phenomenology in early twentieth-century Europe; later thinkers emphasized existence, embodiment, and the social lifeworld.
## 4. Core concepts
Intentionality; first-person experience; lifeworld; embodiment; subject-object relation; bracketing; meaning structure.
## 5. Basic claims
Phenomenology describes how phenomena appear in experience before reducing them to causal explanation. Different schools disagree about method and ontology.
## 6. Research findings
Phenomenology is a philosophical approach and informs qualitative methods; it is not a causal experimental theory. Qualitative rigor depends on transparent sampling, analysis, and reflexivity.
## 7. Classic research
Husserl (1913), *Ideas I*; Heidegger (1927), *Being and Time*; Merleau-Ponty (1945), *Phenomenology of Perception*.
## 8. Scope
Useful for asking what a feeling or situation is like for the user and how it is experienced in their everyday world.
## 9. Limitations
Description alone may not identify causes; introspection is partial and language shapes reports. Avoid claiming direct access to another person's experience.
## 10. Applied example
Ask what “being overlooked” feels like in the user's body, attention, and sense of the room before offering a causal theory of why it occurred.
## 11. Related theories
Existentialism; embodied cognition; qualitative psychology; narrative inquiry; cognitive science.
## 12. References and evidence status
Husserl (1913), *Ideas I* [philosophical method]; Heidegger (1927), *Being and Time* [existential phenomenology]; Merleau-Ponty (1945), *Phenomenology of Perception* [embodied account]. Philosophical tradition, not experimental consensus.
