# Cognitive Science: Information Processing and Metacognition

## 1. Theory name
Information-processing, working-memory, attention, metacognition, and decision research.
## 2. Proponents
George Miller; Ulric Neisser; Alan Baddeley; John Flavell; contemporary cognitive scientists.
## 3. Historical background
The cognitive revolution broadened psychology beyond behaviorist accounts, integrating experimental psychology, linguistics, computation, and neuroscience.
## 4. Core concepts
Attention; encoding; working memory; retrieval; representation; monitoring; confidence; metacognition; cognitive load.
## 5. Basic claims
People use limited-capacity systems to encode and interpret information. Memory is reconstructive; confidence and accuracy can diverge.
## 6. Research findings
Working memory, attention, and memory retrieval show robust experimental effects, but individual performance depends on task, prior knowledge, sleep, stress, and context.
## 7. Classic research
Miller (1956), “The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two,” *Psychological Review*, 63, 81–97 [historical estimate later revised]; Baddeley & Hitch (1974), “Working Memory,” in *The Psychology of Learning and Motivation*.
## 8. Scope
Useful for exploring how attention, recall, and uncertainty shape self-understanding and interpretations of others.
## 9. Limitations
Computer metaphors are models, not literal descriptions of a mind. Laboratory tasks do not capture all lived experience; avoid inferring a hidden mechanism from a single memory.
## 10. Applied example
When recalling an argument, distinguish what was directly heard from what is reconstructed later; note emotional salience and missing context.
## 11. Related theories
Neuroscience; learning; metacognition; decision science; phenomenology.
## 12. References and evidence status
Miller (1956) [historical review, numerical claim revised]; Baddeley & Hitch (1974) [working-memory model]; Neisser (1967), *Cognitive Psychology* [foundational synthesis].
